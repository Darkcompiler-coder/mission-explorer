02 / Mars

Robots that changed our picture of Mars

Mars missions carry instruments to photograph terrain, test soil, measure weather and listen for marsquakes. Some landers have gone silent; a newer generation of rovers is still exploring.

Chronological guide

From the first successful landing to today

Use the mission explorer to filter the full list by date or operating status. “No longer communicating” means that mission operations have ended; it does not mean the spacecraft vanished.

Mission ended

Viking 1 and Viking 2

Chryse Planitia and Utopia Planitia · landed 1976

Each spacecraft combined an orbiter with a lander. The landers photographed the sites, measured weather and used robotic arms to sample soil for chemical and biological experiments.

Legacy: Viking returned the first successful long-running surface observations from Mars. The two landers operated until 1982 and 1980.

Mission ended

Mars Pathfinder and Sojourner

Ares Vallis · landed 1997

Pathfinder delivered a stationary science lander and Sojourner, the first rover to operate on Mars. The rover examined nearby rocks while the lander returned images and weather data.

Legacy: The mission demonstrated airbag-assisted landing and a low-cost approach to robotic surface exploration.

Mission ended

Spirit

Gusev Crater · landed 2004

Spirit was one of NASA’s twin Mars Exploration Rovers. It explored rocks and soil for signs of past water and continued operating for years beyond its original 90-sol plan.

Mission end: Spirit became stuck in soft soil and stopped communicating in 2010.

Mission ended

Opportunity

Meridiani Planum and Endeavour Crater · landed 2004

Opportunity studied rocks and soils for evidence of past water. Designed for 90 sols, it worked for nearly 15 years and travelled about 45 kilometres.

Mission end: A planet-wide dust storm blocked sunlight in 2018. NASA received no further response after attempts to contact the rover.

Mission ended

Phoenix

Vastitas Borealis · landed 2008

Phoenix used a robotic arm to dig into Martian soil and examine samples. It confirmed water ice beneath the surface and carried weather and imaging instruments.

Mission end: The stationary lander was designed for a limited surface mission and stopped operating in 2008.

Mission ended

InSight

Elysium Planitia · landed 2018

InSight studied Mars’s interior. Its seismometer detected marsquakes, and its heat-flow experiment was designed to measure heat moving through the planet.

Mission end: Dust reduced the power available from its solar panels. The mission ended in December 2022.

Flight campaign ended

Ingenuity

Jezero Crater · first flight 2021

The small helicopter tested powered, controlled flight in Mars’s thin atmosphere. It was designed for five flights and completed 72.

Mission end: A hard landing in January 2024 damaged rotor blades and ended the flight campaign.

Not left behind

Some Mars missions are still operating

It is useful to distinguish completed missions from current explorers. NASA’s Curiosity and Perseverance rovers remain active science missions. Mars orbiters also continue to observe the planet and relay communications; orbiting spacecraft are not surface equipment.

MissionTypeWhat it does
CuriosityRoverStudies Gale Crater’s geology and past habitability.
PerseveranceRoverStudies Jezero Crater and collects samples for possible future return.
Mars Reconnaissance OrbiterOrbiterImages the surface, studies the atmosphere and supports communications.

The science behind the story

Why look for water?

Water is central to understanding Mars’s climate history and habitability. Evidence of ancient water does not prove that life existed; it helps scientists identify environments and ask better questions about the planet’s past.
