01 / The Moon

Experiments that stayed on the Moon

Apollo astronauts and robotic missions left scientific equipment at their landing sites. Most instruments eventually stopped working, but passive reflectors still help researchers measure the Earth–Moon distance.

A closer look

Not everything left there is “abandoned.”

Some objects are inactive hardware. Some were built to work without power. The status labels below distinguish those cases, and the mission explorer lists missions in date order.

Crewed missions · 1969–1972

Apollo surface instruments and vehicles

Across Apollo missions 11, 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17, crews deployed cameras, seismometers, magnetometers, heat-flow probes and other experiments. The equipment gathered direct measurements from the lunar surface.

Passive science continues

Laser retroreflector arrays

Apollo 11, 14 and 15 sites · deployed 1969–1971

These arrays of corner-cube mirrors reflect laser pulses sent from Earth. They require no electricity and no active signal from the Moon.

Why it matters: Repeated ranging measurements track the Earth–Moon distance and test gravitational physics.

Experiment ended

Apollo 11 EASEP

Tranquility Base · 1969

The Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package included a passive seismic experiment and a laser-ranging reflector. The seismometer sent data for a short period; the reflector remains useful as a target.

Important detail: Apollo 11’s seismometer and reflector had very different lifetimes, despite being deployed together.

Network ended in 1977

Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Packages

Apollo 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17 landing sites

ALSEP stations were powered instrument groups. Depending on the mission, they included seismometers, heat-flow probes, magnetometers and solar-wind experiments.

What they revealed: Long-running measurements helped scientists study moonquakes, the lunar interior and the space environment around the Moon.

Not operating

Lunar Roving Vehicles

Apollo 15, 16 and 17 sites · 1971–1972

The battery-powered rovers carried astronauts across a wider area, allowing them to inspect rocks and bring samples back to the lunar module. They were parked when each mission ended.

Why it matters: The rovers extended the distance crews could explore on foot.

Not operating

Apollo 11 descent stage

Tranquility Base · landed 1969

The lower half of the lunar module remained at the landing site after the ascent stage took the astronauts back to orbit. It served as the crew’s landing platform and launch base from the surface.

Context: Landing stages are mission hardware, but they are not science instruments.

Robotic exploration

Lunar landers and robotic visitors

Mission ended

Surveyor 3

Oceanus Procellarum · landed 1967

Surveyor 3 tested technologies for a soft lunar landing and studied the surface. Apollo 12 astronauts later visited the lander and returned selected parts to Earth for examination.

Why it matters: The visit offered a rare chance to inspect how equipment changed after years on the Moon.

Mission ended

Lunokhod robotic rovers

Luna 17 and Luna 21 landing sites · 1970 and 1973

The Soviet Union’s Lunokhod 1 and 2 were remote-controlled rovers. They photographed terrain and carried instruments over the lunar surface; both remain at their final locations.

Why it matters: They demonstrated mobile robotic exploration before modern planetary rovers.

Still orbiting

Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter

Orbit around the Moon · launched 2009

LRO is not left on the surface: it continues to orbit the Moon and collect observations. Its images help scientists map landing sites and understand the lunar environment.

Keep the categories clear: An orbiter is different from hardware parked on the surface.

Why hardware lasts

The Moon is an unusually quiet archive

There is no dense atmosphere, rain or flowing water to quickly erase tracks. Micrometeorites and extreme temperature changes still alter the surface over time, so lunar equipment is not perfectly preserved—but it can remain recognizable for a very long time.
